# Chapter 16: Hidden Landmines and Unwritten Rules

> *"The things that get you fired are rarely the things you'd expect."*

This chapter covers what nobody tells you when you join a growth-stage company as a senior outsider. Not strategy. Not process. The invisible tripwires, unwritten codes, and social dynamics that can derail you before you even realize you've stepped wrong.

---

## The Outsider Tax

### What It Is

When you join as a senior hire from outside, you pay an invisible tax: everything you do is interpreted through a harsher lens than insiders get.

| What You Do | How Insiders Are Judged | How Outsiders Are Judged |
|-------------|------------------------|--------------------------|
| Ask a question about history | "They're thorough" | "They haven't done their homework" |
| Propose a change | "They're proactive" | "They're already trying to change things without understanding them" |
| Reference past experience | "They have context from doing this before" | "They keep talking about their old company" |
| Disagree with current approach | "Healthy pushback" | "They think they're better than us" |
| Stay quiet for first month | "They're thoughtful" | "What do they actually do?" |
| Build relationships slowly | "They're building trust" | "They're isolated / not integrating" |

**This tax expires** — typically around month 4-6 if you've been earning credibility. But in the early months, assume the harsher interpretation applies to everything.

### How to Pay It Down Faster

- Demonstrate you've done your homework before speaking (reference something specific you've learned HERE, not FROM BEFORE)
- Find one insider who can vouch for you. Their endorsement signals to the group: "This person is one of us now."
- Show curiosity about history and respect for what came before: "I'm impressed that this system handles X — how did you approach that?"
- Deliver an early win that benefits OTHERS (not just your team). Generosity accelerates acceptance.

## The "Unwritten Org Chart"

### Formal vs. Real Power

The org chart shows reporting lines. It doesn't show:
- **Who the CEO/CTO actually listens to** (often a senior IC or early employee, not a VP)
- **Who has "air rights"** (permission to message executives directly)
- **Who is protected** (someone who seems underperforming but won't be touched — usually has history/relationships)
- **Who is on their way out** (someone who's been sidelined but hasn't left yet)
- **Informal alliances** (two directors who always align — taking on one means taking on both)
- **Sacred cows** (projects, systems, or people that cannot be criticized regardless of merit)

### How to Discover the Real Map

- Watch who speaks first and last in exec meetings (first = sets agenda, last = gets final word)
- Watch who gets interrupted vs. who doesn't (uninterrupted = high status)
- Watch who gets "looped in" on decisions outside their domain (signals broad trust)
- Notice who eats lunch with whom, who carpools, who chats in hallways
- Ask your boss: "Who should I make sure to build relationships with?" (They'll reveal the informal power map)
- Notice who gets budget/headcount even in tight quarters (they have political capital)

### The "Untouchable" Person

Almost every company has someone who:
- Has been there from early days
- Has deep institutional knowledge
- May or may not be performing well currently
- Cannot be criticized, moved, or managed out
- Has a direct relationship with the CEO/founder

**How to handle:** Don't try to "fix" or go around this person. Build a relationship with them. They can be your biggest ally or your biggest blocker — and you choose which by how you treat them. They've seen many senior hires come and go. Show them respect and they'll tell you things no one else will.

## Cultural Landmines at Growth-Stage Companies

### Landmine: Criticizing the Founding Architecture

**The trap:** The architecture is clearly legacy, clearly struggling. You see obvious improvements. You say so in a meeting.

**The explosion:** The person who built that architecture is still here — or their close friend is. They hear "your baby is ugly." Even if they KNOW it needs to change, hearing an outsider say it feels different than admitting it themselves.

**Safe path:** "This architecture has gotten us to $XM in revenue — impressive. As we scale to $YM, we'll need it to evolve. Here's what I'd suggest for the next phase." (Honors the past, builds to the future.)

### Landmine: Moving Faster Than the Culture Allows

**The trap:** You're hired to "bring enterprise discipline" or "scale the team." You take that mandate literally. You start changing things in month 2.

**The explosion:** People feel steamrolled. "They haven't even been here two months and they're rewriting everything." Your boss gets complaints. You're told to "slow down" — and now you're confused because you thought speed was the mandate.

**Safe path:** Even if your mandate is change — the PACE of change must match the culture's absorption rate. Introduce one change at a time. Let each one settle before introducing the next. "Fast" in growth-stage doesn't mean "all at once."

### Landmine: Being Too Close to Your Team, Too Distant from Peers

**The trap:** You focus on your team (comfortable, controllable) and neglect peer relationships (uncomfortable, uncertain).

**The explosion:** After 6 months, your team loves you but peers view you as siloed, uncooperative, or invisible. When your initiatives need cross-functional support — you don't have it.

**Safe path:** Force yourself to invest 30-40% of your relationship-building time LATERALLY (peers) and UPWARD (boss, boss's peers), not just downward (your team).

### Landmine: The "Feedback in Public" Misstep

**The trap:** You provide constructive feedback to someone in a group setting, intending to be helpful and transparent.

**The explosion:** In many growth-stage cultures (especially those with high psychological safety anxiety), any correction in public feels like humiliation. The person shuts down. Others note: "This new Director calls people out publicly."

**Safe path:** Praise in public. Correct in private. Always. The only exception: if someone is about to make a decision that will cause immediate harm, and there's no time for a sidebar.

### Landmine: Email/Slack Tone Misread

**The trap:** You write a brief, direct message. "Let's not do that. Here's what I'd suggest instead."

**The explosion:** Intended as efficient and clear. Read as dismissive, cold, or imperious. Written communication strips tone — and new people don't get the benefit of the doubt on tone.

**Safe path:** In the first 3 months, err on the side of warmer than feels natural. Add a "Thanks for flagging this" or "Good thinking — here's a different angle" before the redirect. Once people know you, you can be more direct.

## Things Nobody Tells You About Being Senior in a New Place

### You Will Feel Lonely

At Director level in a new company:
- You can't be fully vulnerable with your team (they need you to be steady)
- You don't know your peers well enough yet to be honest
- Your boss is evaluating you (not yet a confidant)
- Your old company friends are moving on without you
- Your family sees you stressed but can't relate to the specifics

**This is normal.** It's not a sign you made the wrong choice. Build a support system deliberately: external mentor, executive coach, Director-level peers at other companies, one or two trusted people inside the company (takes 3-6 months to identify).

### The "Honeymoon Compliment" Trap

In your first 2-3 months, people will say nice things:
- "Great to have you!" 
- "You're ramping so fast!"
- "Love what you're doing with the team!"

**Don't believe it uncritically.** This is the honeymoon period. People are being welcoming. The REAL feedback starts at month 4-6, when the politeness tax has expired and people start evaluating you on substance.

**What to do:** Appreciate the warmth but don't mistake it for earned credibility. Keep working as if you haven't proven anything yet — because you haven't.

### Your Reputation from Before Means Nothing Here

Whatever you accomplished at your previous company — the systems you built, the teams you grew, the promotions you earned — starts at zero here. People may know your background from your intro, but they'll form their opinion entirely from what they observe directly.

**The liberation:** You can be whoever you want to be. Old reputations (good or bad) don't follow.

**The weight:** You have to earn it all again. No shortcuts.

### The First "No" You Get

At some point, something you want will be denied. Budget. Headcount. A project you wanted to lead. A decision that goes against you.

**How you respond to your first "no" defines your reputation:**
- Graceful acceptance + regrouping = mature leader who picks battles wisely
- Visible frustration or persistent lobbying = someone who can't take no for an answer
- Going around the person who said no = political saboteur (even if you frame it as "getting more perspectives")
- Sulking/withdrawing = someone who's not resilient

The first "no" is a test. Pass it.

## Relationship Landmines

### The "Former Candidate" Trap

Someone internally may have wanted your job. They didn't get it. Now they report to you, or they're a peer. They might be:
- Cooperative (genuinely moved on)
- Passively resistant (doing minimum, waiting for you to fail)
- Actively undermining (rarely, but it happens)

**How to detect:** Watch for: slow-rolling your requests, "forgetting" to include you, subtle corrections in front of others, comments like "we tried that before" on everything you propose.

**How to handle:** Address directly and early. "I know you were considered for this role. I want to understand your perspective and find ways to work well together. What would make this work for you?" Give them a path to dignity. Most people just want to feel valued.

### The "Over-Alignment" Trap with Your Boss

**The trap:** You align SO closely with your boss that you become perceived as their "lieutenant" rather than an independent leader. You always agree with them publicly. You echo their language. You check with them before stating positions.

**The problem:** Peers stop coming to you directly (they go to your boss). Your team sees you as a pass-through, not a leader. Your value is "being aligned" rather than "having independent judgment."

**The fix:** Agree with your boss OFTEN (they're usually right, and alignment matters). But occasionally, publicly and respectfully hold a different position. "I actually see this differently than [boss] — here's my take." This signals independent thinking and builds credibility as a leader in your own right.

### The "Inherited Team Loyalty" Problem

Your inherited team had a previous leader. They may have loved that person. You are not that person.

**What you'll face:**
- Comparisons (explicit or implicit): "Under [previous leader], we did it this way..."
- Testing your boundaries: "Can we get away with what the old boss allowed?"
- Loyalty testing: "Are you going to keep the team/culture the same?"
- Grief: people legitimately miss someone they worked well with

**How to handle:**
- Never criticize the previous leader (even if they were terrible)
- Acknowledge what worked: "I hear great things about how [previous leader] handled X. I want to build on that."
- But don't try to BE them. You're different. Own that: "I may do some things differently. That's not a criticism of what came before — it's just who I am."
- Give people time. Loyalty transfers slowly. Don't demand it; earn it.

## Things That Will Surprise You

### How Slowly Trust Builds

In enterprise, trust comes with title and tenure. In growth-stage, trust comes only from: demonstrated competence + demonstrated reliability + demonstrated good intent. This takes 4-6 months minimum. Don't get frustrated that people don't trust your judgment at month 2.

### How Fast Trust Breaks

One bad interaction, one broken commitment, one leaked confidence — trust takes months to build and can break in a day. Guard it.

| Trust Builder | Time to Build | Trust Breaker | Time to Destroy |
|--------------|---------------|---------------|-----------------|
| Consistent delivery over a quarter | 3 months | One missed commitment with no warning | 1 day |
| Always keeping confidences | 6 months | Repeating something said in confidence once | Instant |
| Giving credit to others | Ongoing | Taking credit for someone's work once | Instant |
| Being honest even when it's hard | 6 months | One lie discovered (even small) | Instant |

### How Much People Talk About You (Without You Knowing)

You're a new senior leader. People discuss you:
- "What's the new Director like?"
- "Did you hear what they said in that meeting?"
- "Are they any good?"
- "How long do you think they'll last?"

This isn't gossip — it's how organizations make sense of change. You can't control it. But you can control what they have to talk about: give them evidence of competence, character, and care. That's what will circulate.

### How Little Formality Matters (and How Much Consistency Matters)

Growth-stage companies don't care if you use the right template, follow the perfect process, or write the most polished document. They care if you:
- Show up consistently
- Do what you say you'll do
- Respond when needed
- Make things better over time
- Don't create drama

Consistency > polish. Always.

## The "New Director" Checklist of Don'ts (First 6 Months)

| Don't | Why |
|-------|-----|
| Don't reorg in the first 90 days | You don't understand enough yet. And it signals "nothing you built matters." |
| Don't fire anyone in month 1-2 (barring egregious conduct) | You don't have enough context to know if it's the person or the situation. |
| Don't bring in people from your old company immediately | Signals you don't trust the people here. Wait until you've built credibility. |
| Don't change tools/systems in the first quarter | Signals you prioritize your comfort over understanding. |
| Don't compare out loud ("at my old company...") | Ever. Even when relevant. Frame insights without the source attribution. |
| Don't skip social events/team lunches repeatedly | You're building relationships. This is work, not optional. |
| Don't CC your boss on everything | Signals you're either covering yourself or escalating passively. |
| Don't send late-night emails/Slacks regularly | Sets an expectation you can't sustain, and pressures your team. |
| Don't volunteer for everything | Focus > breadth. Being everywhere = being nowhere. |
| Don't bad-mouth the company's past decisions | You weren't here. You don't know the constraints they faced. |

## Chapter Summary

The hidden landmines are the things that can hurt you BEFORE you demonstrate your strategic or operational value. They're social, cultural, and relational — not technical. The outsider tax is real but temporary. The unwritten org chart matters more than the formal one. Cultural landmines explode when you violate norms you didn't know existed. Trust builds slowly, breaks instantly, and can only be earned through consistent behavior over time.

**The meta-rule:** In the first 6 months, when in doubt: listen more, assume less, move slower, be warmer, give more credit, and remember that your every action is being watched and interpreted by people who don't yet know your character. Everything you do is evidence. Make sure the evidence tells the right story.
