# Chapter 10: Conclusion

> **The Manager's Path** — Camille Fournier
> *A Guide for Tech Leaders Navigating Growth and Change*

> "Stay curious, and good luck on your path!"

Fournier closes with three principles that thread through the entire book:

## Self-Management Is the Foundation

"You have to be able to manage yourself if you want to be good at managing others." Understanding your reactions, inspirations, and irritations is prerequisite to managing anyone else. This echoes the book's arc: Ch1 starts with how you want to be managed; Ch10 ends with how you manage yourself.

## Conflict Is the Core Skill

"Great managers are masters of working through conflict." The key: taking your ego out of the conversation, seeing past your interpretations and stories. "If you want to be able to tell people hard things and have them hear what you have to say, you must be able to tell them without embellishing the facts with your storyline."

Her personal practices: meditation (recommended: Tara Brach podcasts, Pema Chödrön writings) and daily morning freewriting, always ending with the mantra **"Get curious."**

## Curiosity Above All

Her coach's consistent advice across every difficult situation: think about the other person's perspective. What are they trying to do? What do they value? What do they want and need?

"Apply that curiosity to people. Apply it to process. Apply it to technology, and strategy, and business. Ask questions, and be willing to have your notions proven wrong."

> **[Insight]** The book ends where it should: not with a framework or a checklist, but with a disposition. Curiosity is the meta-skill that makes all other management skills work. The curious manager runs better 1-1s (they ask real questions). The curious leader debugs organizations (they chase the "why"). The curious executive builds strategy (they explore possibilities). Every chapter's advice improves when applied with genuine curiosity.

> **[SRE Lens: Curiosity Is the SRE Superpower]**
>
> Blameless postmortems work because of curiosity — asking "why did the system behave this way?" instead of "who caused this?" Incident response works because of curiosity — "what else could explain this behavior?" SLO design works because of curiosity — "what do our customers actually need?" The best SRE leaders are the most curious ones — about systems, about people, about organizations, and about themselves.

## The SRE Leadership Operating Manual: Key Takeaways

Looking back across all chapters, the principles most critical for SRE leadership:

1. **Enable focus** (Ch1) — protect your teams from reactive work overwhelming project work
2. **Stay technical** (Ch5-6) — you can't lead what you don't understand; incidents are your code reviews
3. **Be kind, not nice** (Ch5) — direct feedback saves careers; avoidance destroys them
4. **Build psychological safety** (Ch5) — blameless postmortems aren't just process, they're culture
5. **Data-driven decisions** (Ch5) — SRE teams have the best operational data; use it to influence
6. **Delegate to develop** (Ch6) — teach your people to do your job so you can do your next job
7. **First-team focus** (Ch6-8) — your peers are your first team, not your direct reports
8. **Hold managers accountable** (Ch7) — bring me clear problems, not hidden fires
9. **Set True North** (Ch8) — define what excellence looks like and let it guide the organization
10. **Get curious** (Ch10) — about systems, about people, about yourself
