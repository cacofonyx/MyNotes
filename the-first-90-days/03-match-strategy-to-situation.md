# Chapter 3: Match Strategy to Situation

> *"If you don't understand the type of situation you're in, you can't know what approach will work."*

There is no one-best-way to lead change. The biggest mistake leaders make is applying the same approach to every situation — the turnaround artist who tries radical surgery on a healthy organization, or the consensus builder who moves too slowly in a crisis. This chapter introduces the **STARS model** — a diagnostic framework for identifying what type of business situation you're inheriting, so you can match your strategy, leadership style, learning focus, and pace to the actual demands you face.

The STARS model (Start-up, Turnaround, Accelerated growth, Realignment, Sustaining success) is the most referenced framework in the entire book. Every subsequent chapter's advice — on learning, early wins, team building, alliances — should be filtered through your STARS diagnosis. What works in a turnaround can destroy you in a realignment.

The chapter also introduces the **hero vs. steward** leadership style continuum and the uncomfortable truth that the most challenging situations (realignment, sustaining success) are the least preferred and hardest to get recognized for.

## Case Study: Karl Lewin

> **[Case Study: Karl Lewin — Same Person, Two Different Situations, Two Different Approaches]**
>
> **Context:** Karl is a hard-driving executive at Global Foods (multinational consumer products). First assignment: turnaround of European manufacturing. Second assignment: EVP of supply chain for core North American operations.
>
> **Europe (Turnaround):**
> - Organization was broken — overemphasis on acquisitions, fragmented country-level ops
> - Karl acted decisively: centralized functions, closed 4 inefficient plants, shifted production to Eastern Europe
> - Painful cuts bore fruit within 18 months — dramatic efficiency improvement
> - Classic "hero" leadership: quick diagnosis, bold action, command-and-control
>
> **North America (Realignment):**
> - NOT in immediate crisis — long-term success only recently showing signs of slipping
> - Benchmarks: slightly below average efficiency, lower third in customer satisfaction
> - Problems were real but subtle: fire-fighting addiction, gut-feeling decisions, poor information systems, unfounded optimism
> - Karl recognized: this is NOT a turnaround. If he applied turnaround tactics (radical surgery), he'd trigger massive resistance from an organization that doesn't believe it's broken.
>
> **What Karl did differently in North America:**
> - Focused on cultural/political learning (not just technical assessment)
> - Made slower, more deliberate moves (not radical surgery)
> - Focused on systems, skills, and culture (not strategy and structure)
> - Promoted from within (not external recruitment)
> - Early win was raising AWARENESS of the problem (using metrics, external benchmarks, consultant assessments) — not solving the problem
> - Built alliances sideways and down (not just from the top)
>
> **The lesson:** Same leader, equally effective in both — because he DIAGNOSED the situation and adjusted his approach. Had he charged in to North America with his Europe playbook, he would have alienated an organization that was fundamentally capable but drifting.

## The STARS Model

> **[Core Framework: STARS — Five Types of Business Situations]**
>
> STARS = **S**tart-up, **T**urnaround, **A**ccelerated growth, **R**ealignment, **S**ustaining success
>
> Every new role places you in one (or a mix) of these situations. Each demands fundamentally different strategies, leadership styles, and pacing.
>
> | Situation | What it IS | Key challenges | Key opportunities | Organizational psychology |
> |-----------|-----------|----------------|-------------------|---------------------------|
> | **Start-up** | Assembling capabilities from scratch to get something new off the ground (new business, product, team, initiative) | Building strategy/structure/systems from nothing. Recruiting a team. Making do with limited resources. No existing framework to build on. | You can do things RIGHT from the start. No legacy constraints. People are energized by possibility. | **Excited confusion.** Your job: channel energy into productive directions by deciding what NOT to do. |
> | **Turnaround** | Saving a business/initiative that everyone acknowledges is in serious trouble. The "burning platform." | Reenergizing demoralized people. Making tough decisions under time pressure. Going deep enough with painful cuts. | Everyone recognizes change is necessary (no denial to overcome). External support available. A little success goes a long way with demoralized people. | **Despair.** Your job: provide a concrete plan and confidence that things will improve. |
> | **Accelerated growth** | Managing rapid expansion of something that's started to hit its stride | Putting in structures/processes/systems to permit scaling. Integrating many new hires while preserving culture. Risk of expanding too much too fast. | Growth potential motivates people. People willing to stretch. Strong foundation exists. | **Energized but stretched.** Your job: help people accept that MORE DISCIPLINE is needed, and get them working within defined processes. |
> | **Realignment** | Reenergizing something that was once successful but is now drifting toward trouble. The clouds are gathering but the storm hasn't broken. | Convincing people that change is NECESSARY (they may not see the problem). Carefully restructuring top team. Overcoming denial and complacency. | Organization has significant pockets of strength. People want to continue seeing themselves as successful. | **Denial.** Your job: pierce the veil — open eyes to the fact that a problem actually exists. |
> | **Sustaining success** | Preserving the vitality of a successful organization and taking it to the next level | Living in the shadow of the previous leader. Playing good defense before too many new initiatives. Finding ways to take it higher. | Strong team may already be in place. Motivated people with a history of success. Foundation for continued growth may exist. | **Complacency.** Your job: invent the challenge — find ways to keep people motivated and combat drift. |
>
> **Critical insight: the eventual GOAL is the same for all five** — a successful, growing business. But the PATH differs enormously.
>
> **Key distinction between the five:**
> - **Start-ups, turnarounds, and accelerated growth** = resource-intensive construction work. Less existing infrastructure. You get to build (or rebuild) relatively freely. More action-oriented, authority-driven.
> - **Realignments and sustaining success** = significant existing strengths AND serious constraints. You have time but need to use it wisely for learning, reflection, and influence-building. Disrupting what works will cost you dearly.

> **[SRE/Platform Leader Lens: Which STARS Situation Are You Walking Into?]**
>
> For your specific transition (enterprise SRE → growth-stage IGA company as senior director), here's how to diagnose your STARS situation:
>
> | If you see this... | It's probably... | Implication |
> |-------------------|-----------------|-------------|
> | No platform team existed before you. You're building from scratch. | **Start-up** | You get to shape everything — but you also have to build credibility, recruit, and define scope from nothing. Exciting but exhausting. |
> | Platform/infra is in crisis: constant outages, customers leaving, on-call is destroying people, technical debt is crippling the business | **Turnaround** | Move fast. Make tough calls. People will follow you because they're desperate for improvement. But don't mistake urgency for license to skip learning. |
> | The company is growing fast, platform needs to scale dramatically, but the foundations exist and basically work | **Accelerated growth** | Your job is to add discipline, structure, and processes WITHOUT killing the agility that got the company here. The team is stretched but motivated. |
> | Platform exists, once worked well, but is gradually drifting — increasing incidents, slowing deploys, accumulating debt, yet nobody feels URGENT pain | **Realignment** | The HARDEST situation. Your biggest challenge is making people SEE the problem. The org doesn't think it's broken — they think it's "fine." You need data, benchmarks, and patience. |
> | Platform is solid, team is strong, the previous leader did a good job. You're there to take it to the next level. | **Sustaining success** | Don't break what works. Understand deeply what made it successful. Find the NEXT challenge — not a reinvention of the current success. |
>
> **Most likely for your transition:** Some blend of **accelerated growth** (company is growing, platform needs to scale) and **realignment** (platform practices may have drifted as the company grew faster than the platform could mature). This is actually the hardest blend — you need to simultaneously ADD structure AND pierce denial about what's not working.
>
> **The trap:** You may instinctively treat it as a turnaround (because it LOOKS like one from your enterprise-standards perspective). But if the organization doesn't FEEL like it's in crisis — because they've been successfully serving customers despite what you see as chaos — you'll trigger the immune response. Match your approach to THEIR reality, not your assessment.

> **[Questions to Ask: Diagnosing Your STARS Situation]**
>
> These questions help you determine which situation you're actually in:
>
> **Ask your boss:**
> - "On a scale of crisis to thriving, where do you think the platform/SRE function is right now?" (Their answer calibrates urgency)
> - "What's the most important thing — stabilize what exists, or build new capabilities?" (Realignment vs. start-up)
> - "How much change appetite does the organization have right now?" (Turnaround = high, realignment = low)
>
> **Ask your team:**
> - "Do you feel like things are getting better or worse over the past year?" (Trajectory matters more than current state)
> - "When was the last time you felt genuinely proud of something the team accomplished?" (If recent = sustaining success vibes. If they can't remember = possible turnaround morale.)
> - "What's stopping you from doing your best work?" (Reveals whether constraints are resource/structural vs. cultural/political)
>
> **Ask yourself:**
> - "If I do nothing for 6 months, will things get significantly worse — or just stay roughly the same?" (Turnaround = yes, gets worse fast. Realignment = slow drift. Sustaining success = things continue fine.)
> - "Is there widespread agreement that change is needed?" (Yes = turnaround. No = realignment.)

## Diagnosing Your STARS Portfolio

> **[Core Framework: The STARS Portfolio — It's Usually a Mix]**
>
> Pure STARS situations are rare. In reality, you'll manage a **portfolio** of products, projects, processes, and people — each in a different STARS state simultaneously.
>
> **How to use the STARS portfolio diagnostic:**
>
> | STARS situation | Which part of my responsibility fits here? | Priority % of my effort in next 90 days |
> |----------------|-------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------|
> | Start-up | ___ | ___% |
> | Turnaround | ___ | ___% |
> | Accelerated growth | ___ | ___% |
> | Realignment | ___ | ___% |
> | Sustaining success | ___ | ___% |
> | **Total** | | **100%** |
>
> **Warning on preferences:** If the situation you most PREFER to work in (probably start-up or turnaround — they're the most exciting) is also what you assigned the highest priority, check yourself. Are your preferences influencing your diagnosis? You might be unconsciously seeing a turnaround because you WANT to play hero — when the reality is a realignment that needs patient stewardship.

> **[SRE/Platform Leader Lens: Sample STARS Portfolio for IGA Platform]**
>
> A growth-stage IGA company's platform/SRE might look like:
>
> | STARS situation | Possible elements | Priority |
> |----------------|------------------|----------|
> | **Start-up** | Platform engineering function itself (if the team/discipline is new). Possibly: new connector framework, new observability layer | 20% |
> | **Turnaround** | On-call health (if pages > 5/week). Specific systems in crisis. Specific customer reliability problems. | 15% |
> | **Accelerated growth** | Core infrastructure that works but needs scaling. Team size that needs to grow. Processes that worked for 5 people but break at 20. | 35% |
> | **Realignment** | Platform practices that drifted (no SLOs, informal incident response, growing tech debt nobody wants to acknowledge). Culture that prizes speed over reliability. | 25% |
> | **Sustaining success** | Things that work well and should be protected (maybe a well-functioning CI pipeline, or a strong team culture) | 5% |
>
> **Implication:** You need to simultaneously be a hero (for the turnaround parts), a builder (for the start-up parts), a process installer (for the accelerated growth parts), AND a patient influencer (for the realignment parts). This is why the job is hard — and why different parts need different approaches.

## Leading Change Differently by Situation

> **[Core Framework: Six Activities Adapted by STARS Situation]**
>
> The same activities (learning, strategy, priorities, team, wins, alliances) must be executed DIFFERENTLY depending on your situation:
>
> | Activity | In a TURNAROUND | In a REALIGNMENT |
> |----------|----------------|------------------|
> | **1. Learning** | Focus on TECHNICAL learning (strategy, markets, technologies). Prepare to act quickly. | Focus on CULTURAL and POLITICAL learning. Prepare to act deliberately. |
> | **2. Strategy** | Prune non-core. Cut to a defensible core. | Hone and leverage existing capabilities. Stimulate innovation. |
> | **3. Priorities** | Faster, bolder moves. Focus on strategy and structure. | Slower, more deliberate moves. Focus on systems, skills, and culture. |
> | **4. Team** | Clean house at the top. Recruit external talent. | Make a few high-payoff changes. Promote from within. |
> | **5. Early wins** | Shift mindset from **despair to hope** (show things CAN improve) | Shift mindset from **denial to awareness** (show things NEED to improve) |
> | **6. Alliances** | Gain support from bosses/stakeholders for resources | Build alliances sideways and down for execution |
>
> **Karl's early wins illustrate the difference perfectly:**
> - Europe (turnaround): closed failing plants, shifted production → showed action, gave people hope that leadership was decisive
> - North America (realignment): introduced external benchmarks, revamped performance metrics → showed DATA, pierced unfounded optimism, made the problem visible without declaring anyone incompetent

> **[SRE/Platform Leader Lens: Leading Change in Your Specific Mix]**
>
> Since you likely face accelerated growth + realignment:
>
> | Activity | What this means for you |
> |----------|------------------------|
> | **Learning** | Heavy emphasis on cultural/political learning (who has power, what's valued, where the denial lives). Technical learning matters but isn't the blocker at director level — your team can teach you the systems. |
> | **Strategy** | Don't prune — BUILD. But build on existing strengths. Identify what the platform already does well and extend it, rather than replacing things that (mostly) work. |
> | **Priorities** | Resist the urge for fast/bold moves. Move deliberately. Your first priority should be a systems/skills/culture improvement (not a structural reorg). |
> | **Team** | Don't clean house. Evaluate carefully. Make maybe 1-2 changes that send a signal — but promote from within where possible. The team knows the domain you're learning. |
> | **Early wins** | You need BOTH: something that delivers hope (a visible improvement) AND something that raises awareness (data showing where the platform is falling short vs. where it needs to be). |
> | **Alliances** | Build sideways (peer directors, product VPs, CISO) and down (key senior engineers whose respect you need). Your boss's support is necessary but not sufficient. |

> **[Trap/Anti-Pattern: Treating a Realignment Like a Turnaround]**
>
> The most common mistake for technically strong leaders entering a growth-stage company:
>
> **What you SEE (through enterprise-trained eyes):** No SLOs. Manual deploys. Alert fatigue. No postmortem culture. Fragile architecture. → This looks like a CRISIS that needs TURNAROUND.
>
> **What THEY see:** A platform that's been successfully serving enterprise customers. Growth. Revenue. Happy customers (mostly). A team that's proud of what they built. → This looks like SUCCESS that could be BETTER.
>
> **The disconnect:** Your diagnosis is "turnaround" but the organization's self-perception is "we're doing fine." If you act like a turnaround leader (radical surgery, external hires, declaring things broken), you'll trigger massive resistance because:
> - People don't feel desperate (turnaround prerequisite: people want saving)
> - People DO feel pride (you're insulting their work)
> - You don't have the political capital yet for bold moves
>
> **The fix:** Even if your technical assessment says "this needs radical improvement," your APPROACH must be realignment-style:
> - Use data and benchmarks to raise awareness (not declarations of failure)
> - Be patient — let the data convince people before you propose solutions
> - Acknowledge strengths before highlighting gaps
> - Make it their realization, not your pronouncement
>
> **The timing:** You can move faster once awareness is established (maybe by month 3-4). But trying to move at turnaround speed from day 1 in a realignment situation is how David Jones at Energix (Ch01) failed.

## Hero vs. Steward: Managing Yourself by Situation

> **[Core Framework: Hero vs. Steward Leadership Styles]**
>
> **Hero** = charging against the enemy, sword in hand. Rapid diagnosis, aggressive moves, command-and-control. People follow because they're desperate for direction.
> - Natural fit for: turnarounds, start-ups
> - Danger zone: realignments and sustaining success (you break things that didn't need breaking)
>
> **Steward** = diplomatic, less ego-driven, builds consensus. Deep cultural/political understanding. Patient and systematic about what to preserve vs. discard. Cultivates awareness of need for change through shared diagnosis.
> - Natural fit for: realignments, sustaining success
> - Danger zone: turnarounds (you move too slowly and waste precious time building consensus nobody needs)
>
> | If you're naturally a... | And your situation is... | Risk | Mitigation |
> |-------------------------|------------------------|------|------------|
> | **Hero** | Realignment | Moving too fast, causing unnecessary resistance | Stock your team with natural stewards. Delegate outreach. Channel heroic energy toward specific parts that ARE in turnaround. |
> | **Hero** | Sustaining success | Breaking what works in search of excitement | Find the NEXT frontier (new capabilities, next-level scale) to channel your energy. Don't set fires just to fight them. |
> | **Steward** | Turnaround | Moving too slowly, wasting time on consensus that nobody needs | Identify the 2-3 decisions that MUST happen now regardless of buy-in. Force yourself to act decisively there. Build consensus everywhere else. |
> | **Steward** | Start-up | Over-deliberating when speed is more important than perfection | Set firm deadlines for decisions. "We'll decide by Friday even with imperfect info." |

> **[Self-Assessment: Hero or Steward?]**
>
> | Indicator | Hero tendency | Steward tendency |
> |-----------|--------------|------------------|
> | When you see a problem, your first instinct is... | Fix it NOW. Decide and move. | Understand it first. Ask who else it affects. |
> | How you feel about consensus-building... | "Meetings where nothing is decided frustrate me." | "Getting alignment upfront saves pain later." |
> | How you react to ambiguity... | "I'll make a call and adjust." | "I'll gather more data before committing." |
> | What energizes you... | Crisis, urgency, decisive action, visible impact | Quiet improvement, system design, preventing problems |
> | How you measure your own success... | "Things changed because I was here." | "Things got better — maybe people don't even realize why." |
>
> **For your transition:** Coming from an enterprise engineering management background, you may have steward INSTINCTS (process-oriented, data-driven, deliberate) but HERO PRESSURE (the organization hired you to make visible change, your boss expects impact, you feel you need to prove yourself at a new level). The danger: forcing yourself into hero mode when steward mode is what the situation requires. Trust your instincts toward deliberateness — BUT be ready to play hero for the specific pieces that truly are in turnaround (e.g., a system that's causing customer escalations right now).

> **[Comparison: Neff & Citrin — The First 100 Days Grid]**
>
> Neff's framework complements STARS with a simpler 2x2:
>
> - **What needs to continue?** (Don't break it)
> - **What needs to change?** (But change how — fast or slow?)
> - **What's urgent?** (Act now — hero mode)
> - **What's important but not urgent?** (Build toward — steward mode)
>
> Neff's key insight: most leaders overweight "urgent" and underweight "important but not urgent." In a realignment, almost everything that MATTERS is important-but-not-urgent. The few genuinely urgent items (if any) deserve hero energy. Everything else deserves patient stewardship.

## Rewarding Success: The Realignment Paradox

> **[Organizational Reality: Why Realignment Is Hardest but Least Rewarded]**
>
> Data from the Harvard Business Review Transition Survey:
>
> | STARS situation | Rated "most challenging" | Rated "most preferred" |
> |----------------|------------------------|-----------------------|
> | Start-up | 13.5% | **47.1%** ← by far most desired |
> | Turnaround | 21.9% | 16.7% |
> | Accelerated growth | 11.6% | 16.1% |
> | Realignment | **30.3%** ← rated hardest | 12.7% |
> | Sustaining success | 22.6% | 7.4% ← nobody wants this |
>
> **The paradox:** The hardest situations (realignment, sustaining success = 52.9% of "most challenging" votes) are the least preferred (20.1% of "most preferred" votes). Why?
>
> 1. **Visibility:** A successful turnaround or start-up is a clear, measurable, individual accomplishment. A successful realignment means... nothing bad happened. It's "the dog that didn't bark" — invisible success.
>
> 2. **Recognition:** Turnaround heroes get celebrated. Stewards who quietly prevent disaster get overlooked. "Nobody calls their power company to say 'thanks for keeping the lights on today.'"
>
> 3. **The measurement problem:** In a realignment, you can't easily answer "compared to what?" Would things have been worse without you? How much worse? There's no counterfactual (an alternative scenario that would have happened without your intervention — you can't prove what WOULD have happened if you hadn't acted).
>
> **Why this matters for you specifically:** As a platform leader, your work is STRUCTURALLY in "sustaining success" / "realignment" territory — keeping infrastructure running, preventing incidents, gradually improving systems. This means your work will ALWAYS be less visible than product leaders who ship features. You must actively manage visibility (Platform Engineering Ch07: "Wins and Challenges") or your value will be invisible to stakeholders — regardless of how much you actually accomplish.

> **[SRE/Platform Leader Lens: The Realignment Paradox in Platform Engineering]**
>
> Platform/SRE work is inherently "realignment" and "sustaining success" — and therefore inherently hard to get recognition for:
>
> | What you accomplish | How it's perceived |
> |--------------------|--------------------|
> | You prevented 5 major outages through proactive improvements | Nobody noticed because the outages didn't happen |
> | You reduced deploy time from 2 hours to 15 minutes | Product team ships faster but doesn't attribute it to you |
> | You migrated to a new database without downtime | "So... nothing happened? Great, moving on." |
> | On-call burden dropped from 10 pages/week to 2 | Your team is happier but leadership sees no external change |
>
> **How to make realignment work visible:**
> - **Quantify the counterfactual:** "Without this investment, based on growth trends, we would have hit X failure by month Y"
> - **Before/after metrics:** "Deploy time was 2 hours. Now it's 15 minutes. That's 200 engineer-hours/month returned to product work."
> - **Customer impact framing:** "Zero customer-impacting incidents during the database migration that touched 200TB of identity data"
> - **External benchmarks:** "Our MTTR improved from 45 min to 12 min — industry benchmark for our size is 30 min"
> - **Wins & Challenges biweekly updates** (from Platform Engineering Ch07) — force visibility into leadership communication

## Closing the Loop

Your STARS diagnosis will deepen and shift as you learn more. Return to this chapter monthly to reassess — especially as you discover that different PARTS of your responsibility are in different STARS states.

> **[Checklist: Match Strategy to Situation]**
>
> 1. What portfolio of STARS situations have you inherited? Which parts are start-up, turnaround, accelerated growth, realignment, sustaining success?
> 2. What are the implications for challenges, opportunities, and how you should approach your transition?
> 3. What are the implications for your learning agenda? (Technical learning? Or culture/politics more critical?)
> 4. What is the prevailing organizational psychology? What transformation do you need to make, and how?
> 5. How can you best lead change given the situation?
> 6. Which of your skills/strengths will be most valuable — and which could get you in trouble?
> 7. What are the implications for the team you need?
> 8. Are you naturally a hero or a steward? Does the situation need the opposite?

> **[Real-World Application: Your STARS Diagnosis — Write It Down]**
>
> Before day 30, write a one-page STARS diagnosis:
>
> 1. **Overall situation type** (your best current assessment)
> 2. **Portfolio breakdown** (which parts are which, with % effort allocation)
> 3. **Organizational psychology** (what do people FEEL — denial? energy? despair? complacency?)
> 4. **Implications for my approach** (what pace? what style? what first moves?)
> 5. **My natural tendency** (hero or steward?) and what adjustment is needed
> 6. **Biggest risk** (what trap am I most likely to fall into given my temperament vs. this situation?)
>
> Share this with your boss. It shows sophisticated situational awareness, opens a conversation about alignment on approach, and creates accountability for you to behave accordingly.
