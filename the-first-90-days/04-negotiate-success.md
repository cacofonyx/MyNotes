# Chapter 4: Negotiate Success

> *"I want to operate on a 90-day time frame, starting with 30 days to get on top of things. Then I will bring you a detailed assessment and plan with goals and actions for the next 60 days."* — Michael Chen to his new boss

No other single relationship matters more than the one with your boss. Your new boss sets benchmarks, interprets your actions for other key players, and controls access to resources. They will have more impact than any other individual on how quickly you reach break-even — and on your eventual success or failure.

Negotiating success means **proactively shaping the game** — establishing realistic expectations, reaching consensus on diagnosis, and securing sufficient resources. Many leaders just PLAY the game reactively, taking their situation as given. This chapter shows how to SHAPE it through five structured conversations with your boss: situation, expectations, resources, style, and personal development.

Critical nuance: what you need from your boss depends on your STARS situation (Ch03). In a realignment, you need help making the case for change. In accelerated growth, you need investment. In a turnaround, you need authority for tough calls.

## Case Study: Michael Chen and Vaughan Cates

> **[Case Study: Michael Chen — Proactively Managing a Difficult Boss]**
>
> **Context:** Michael promoted to CIO for a business unit. His new boss Vaughan Cates has a reputation for being hard-driving, tough on people. Colleagues warned him: "Start updating your résumé."
>
> **The mismatch:** Michael is a planner and team builder. Vaughan expects aggression and fast decisions. Colleagues predicted: "She'll think you're too slow."
>
> **What Michael did (smart moves):**
> 1. **Negotiated a timeline:** Told Vaughan upfront: "30 days to diagnose, then a detailed plan with goals for the next 60 days." Set the frame BEFORE pressure built.
> 2. **Updated regularly:** Kept Vaughan informed of learning progress so silence didn't feel like inaction.
> 3. **Held firm on timeline:** When pressed to make a major systems purchase at week 3, Michael stuck to his 30-day diagnosis period. Delivered a strong plan at day 30 that pleased Vaughan.
> 4. **Came prepared for resource requests:** At month 2, asked for headcount with airtight business case. Survived "withering questioning" because he knew his numbers.
> 5. **Addressed style directly (once credibility was established):** At a later meeting: "We have different styles, but I can deliver for you. Judge me on my results, not on how I get them."
>
> **Result:** Took nearly a year, but built a solid, productive relationship. Key: Michael didn't try to become Vaughan. He delivered results in his own style but explicitly negotiated the terms.
>
> **The lesson:** You don't need your boss to like your style. You need them to trust your results. But you must actively negotiate for the space to operate in your style — it won't be given.

## The Fundamentals: Do's and Don'ts

> **[Core Framework: Boss Relationship — Fundamental Rules]**
>
> **DON'TS:**
>
> | Don't | Why | What happens if you do |
> |-------|-----|------------------------|
> | **Don't stay away** | If your boss doesn't reach out, you must. Otherwise: communication gaps that become credibility gaps. | "I gave them rope and they hung themselves." Your freedom feels like abandonment when things go wrong. |
> | **Don't surprise your boss** | Bosses consider it far worse to learn about a problem from someone ELSE than to hear bad news from you directly. | They feel out of control and lose trust in your judgment — not because the problem exists, but because you didn't flag it. |
> | **Don't approach only with problems** | You'll be perceived as someone who creates work for the boss instead of solving things. | "Every time I see their name in my calendar, I know it's bad news." Avoidance follows. |
> | **Don't run your checklist** | Using boss meetings to recite what you've been doing wastes their time. They want: the 2-3 most important things and how they can help. | Boss tunes you out. Starts canceling meetings. You lose your communication channel. |
> | **Don't expect your boss to change** | You must adapt to THEIR style. Not the other way around. This is non-negotiable regardless of how "wrong" their style feels. | You spend energy being frustrated about style instead of delivering results. |
>
> **DO'S:**
>
> | Do | How |
> |----|-----|
> | **Clarify expectations early and often** | Start during interviews. Get bad news on table early. Lower unrealistic expectations upfront. Check back regularly — expectations SHIFT. |
> | **Take 100% responsibility for the relationship** | Assume your boss will NOT reach out, will NOT offer support, will NOT check on you. If they do, great. If they don't, it's on you. |
> | **Negotiate time for diagnosis** | Like Michael: explicitly ask for 30 days to learn before you're expected to have answers. Get agreement on this timeline. Hold them (and yourself) to it. |
> | **Aim for early wins in THEIR priorities** | Whatever YOUR priorities are, figure out what your BOSS cares about most. Get early results THERE. They'll feel ownership of your success. |
> | **Pursue good marks from people your boss trusts** | Your boss's opinion of you = direct interactions + what they hear from trusted others. Be aware of who those trusted others are. |

> **[SRE/Platform Leader Lens: The Boss Relationship in Your Specific Transition]**
>
> Your boss likely hired you because platform/SRE maturity needs improvement. But consider:
>
> - **They may not know exactly what they need.** They know there's pain. They hired a title ("Senior Director, Platform/SRE"). They may not have precise expectations because they haven't had this role before. You need to CO-CREATE expectations, not just discover them.
> - **They're under pressure too.** Your boss has THEIR boss, THEIR peers, THEIR customers. Your platform problems are ONE of their concerns, not their only concern. Frame your needs in terms of THEIR pressures.
> - **They may have tried before.** There might be a previous platform leader who failed, or a failed initiative. Understanding that history (without asking "what went wrong with my predecessor?" directly) is crucial.
> - **"Don't surprise" is extra-critical for platform/infra.** A production outage your boss learns about from their boss or a customer = catastrophic trust loss. Build an information flow that ensures your boss ALWAYS hears it from you first.

## The Five Conversations Framework

> **[Core Framework: Five Conversations with Your New Boss]**
>
> Not five separate meetings — five **threads of dialogue** that weave through your ongoing relationship:
>
> | # | Conversation | Core question | Timing |
> |---|-------------|--------------|--------|
> | 1 | **Situation** | How does my boss see the STARS portfolio I've inherited? | First 1-2 weeks |
> | 2 | **Expectations** | What does success look like? How will I be measured? | First 2-3 weeks |
> | 3 | **Resources** | What do I need to succeed, and what can my boss provide? | After situation + expectations are aligned (weeks 3-6) |
> | 4 | **Style** | How do we best work together day-to-day? | Early, then ongoing |
> | 5 | **Personal development** | How am I doing? What should I develop? | After ~90 days, once relationship is established |
>
> **The logic of the sequence:** You can't negotiate resources until you agree on situation and expectations. You can't discuss personal development until there's enough trust and track record. But style should be surfaced early to prevent friction from accumulating.

### 1. The Situation Conversation

Goal: reach **shared understanding** of business situation, challenges, and opportunities. If you and your boss don't define the situation the same way, you won't get the support you need.

**What to establish:**
- What STARS situation(s) does your boss see? (May differ from YOUR assessment — that's important data)
- How did the organization reach this point?
- What factors make this a challenge?
- What resources within the org can you draw on?

**What your boss needs from you depends on STARS type:**

| Situation | What your boss should provide |
|-----------|-------------------------------|
| Start-up | Help getting resources quickly. Clear goals. Guidance at strategic breakpoints. |
| Turnaround | Support for tough personnel calls. Support changing external image. Help cutting fast enough. |
| Accelerated growth | Support for investment at right rate. Help making case for new systems/structures. |
| Realignment | Help making the case for change (especially if you're from outside — which you are). |
| Sustaining success | Reality testing. Support for playing good defense. Help finding next-level opportunities. |

> **[Questions to Ask: The Situation Conversation with Your Boss]**
>
> - "How do you see the current state of the platform/SRE function? Where on the spectrum from 'crisis' to 'thriving'?"
> - "What do you think got us to this point — both the strengths and the gaps?"
> - "What's the biggest risk if we don't change anything in the next 6 months?"
> - "Are there previous attempts to address these issues that I should know about?"
> - "Who in the broader organization do you think I should partner with early?"
> - "What's the timeline in your mind — when do you expect to see meaningful progress?"

### 2. The Expectations Conversation

Goal: align on what success looks like, how it's measured, and when.

**Key principles:**

**Underpromise and overdeliver.** Bias toward conservative promises. If you deliver more, you delight. If you promise big and miss, you lose credibility — even if you accomplished a lot.

**Identify the untouchables.** Products, facilities, PEOPLE that your boss is proprietary about (emotionally invested in, personally created, loyal to). You do NOT want to propose shutting down your boss's pet project or replacing their trusted ally. Detect these through: boss's personal history, talking to others, watching body language when topics arise.

**Educate your boss.** If their expectations are unrealistic, work to reset them — but carefully. Use data and benchmarks rather than opinions. Frame as "given what I've learned, here's what I believe is achievable in X timeline" not "your expectations are wrong."

**Clarify, clarify, clarify.** Even if you THINK you understand expectations — go back and confirm. Some bosses know what they want but can't articulate it well. Ask the same question different ways. Ambiguity is DANGEROUS — in a conflict about "what was agreed," the tie goes to the boss, not you.

> **[Questions to Ask: The Expectations Conversation]**
>
> - "If we're having this conversation 90 days from now and you're happy with my progress, what would I have accomplished?"
> - "What does success look like at 6 months? At 1 year?"
> - "How will you measure my progress — what metrics or signals matter to you?"
> - "What are the 2-3 things you most want me to focus on FIRST?"
> - "Is there anything you explicitly do NOT want me to change right now?"
> - "What would concern you — what would make you think I'm going off-track?"
> - "Are there commitments already made (to customers, to the board, to peers) that constrain what I can do?"

> **[SRE/Platform Leader Lens: Expectations for a Platform Leader Joining a Growth-Stage Company]**
>
> Common expectation traps:
>
> - **"Fix reliability"** — but what does that mean? Zero incidents? Faster MTTR? SLOs met? You need to pin this down to measurable outcomes.
> - **"Build the platform team"** — does that mean hire 10 people? Or does it mean take the existing team and elevate their practices? Budget and timeline implications are totally different.
> - **"Reduce toil"** — for whom? Engineering? Customers? Both? How would they measure it?
> - **"Make us enterprise-ready"** — this could mean SOC2, or FedRAMP, or just "fewer outages." Clarify.
>
> **The meta-question to ask:** "If I'm wildly successful in this role over 2 years, what's different about the company?" This surfaces the REAL expectation beneath the job description bullet points.

### 3. The Resource Conversation

Goal: negotiate for what you need to meet the expectations you've aligned on.

**The menu approach:** Present options with costs and benefits. "If you want X outcome, I need Y resources. If you want Z outcome (bigger), I need W resources (more). Here's what happens if we invest less: [specific things we can't do]."

**Don't go back too often.** Put as much as possible on the table early. Repeated ask → erodes credibility. If you need time to scope resources, negotiate for THAT time (like Michael did).

**Resources aren't just headcount and budget:**
- Time (permission to spend 30 days diagnosing before acting)
- Air cover (boss publicly supporting your mandate)
- Political backing (boss making the case for change to THEIR peers)
- Access (introductions to key stakeholders, customers, partners)
- Authority (clarity on what decisions you CAN make without asking)

> **[SRE/Platform Leader Lens: Resource Negotiation for Platform Leadership]**
>
> Resources you likely need that aren't obvious:
>
> | Resource | Why you need it | How to ask |
> |----------|----------------|------------|
> | **Time before expectations kick in** | You can't fix reliability in a system you don't understand yet | "I need 30 days to diagnose before I commit to specific outcomes" (Michael's approach) |
> | **Boss's public backing** | In a realignment, people won't change unless they see leadership alignment | "I need you to say publicly that platform maturity is a priority — not just tell me privately" |
> | **Access to customer context** | Platform decisions need product/customer context you don't have | "I need to sit in on 2-3 customer escalation calls and talk with the account team for our biggest customers" |
> | **Authority boundary clarity** | What can you decide alone? What needs approval? | "Can I make personnel decisions (performance management, reorgs) without prior approval, or do you want to be consulted?" |
> | **Budget for tooling** | Enterprise-grade tooling costs real money | Frame as investment: "X tool costs $Y/year but reduces incident MTTR by Z%, which translates to [customer impact reduction]" |

### 4. The Style Conversation

Goal: figure out how to interact day-to-day without unnecessary friction.

**Diagnose your boss's style:**
- Communication preference: email? Slack? face-to-face? Scheduled meetings vs. ad-hoc?
- How often do they want updates? Daily? Weekly? Only when something changes?
- Detail level: big picture only, or want to see the data?
- Decision involvement: what do they want consulted on vs. delegated to you?
- Working hours: do they expect matching availability?

**"Scope out the dimensions of your box"** = understand the boundaries of your decision-making autonomy. Initially, expect a small box. As trust builds, the box should expand. If it doesn't expand after you've delivered results — that's a conversation to have explicitly.

(The "box" = the set of decisions you can make on your own. Outside the box = decisions that need boss approval or consultation. Early in a relationship, nearly everything is outside your box. Over time, your demonstrated judgment earns you a larger box.)

> **[Questions to Ask: The Style Conversation]**
>
> - "How do you prefer to be updated — written summaries? Quick Slack messages? Scheduled 1:1s?"
> - "How often should we meet? What format works best for you?"
> - "When something goes wrong (an incident, a miss), how would you like me to communicate that?"
> - "What kinds of decisions do you want me to bring to you vs. handle myself?"
> - "Is there a level of spend/headcount change I can make without your approval?"
> - "What's the best way to raise a disagreement with you if I think we should go a different direction?"

### 5. The Personal Development Conversation

Timing: ~90 days in, once relationship has some foundation.

**Not a formal performance review** — an open discussion: what's going well, what needs to change, what skills to develop.

Key for your transition specifically: you're making a level jump (manager → director scope). You WILL have gaps. Explicitly asking for feedback on those gaps signals: self-awareness, coachability, and commitment to growth. The willingness to ask = a leadership signal itself.

> **[Comparison: Neff & Citrin — Managing Up at Senior Levels]**
>
> Neff adds nuance for senior hires specifically:
>
> **"The honeymoon is shorter than you think."** At senior levels, patience runs out faster. Your boss (and their stakeholders) expect impact sooner. Use the honeymoon period to learn — but be aware it has an expiration date.
>
> **"Your boss is interviewing you every day."** Every interaction is still an evaluation in the first 90 days. The way you frame a problem, the questions you ask, the maturity of your thinking — all are being assessed. Be intentional about what each interaction signals.
>
> **"Don't mistake access for alignment."** Having regular meetings with your boss doesn't mean you're aligned. Alignment = your boss can articulate your priorities accurately to THEIR boss. Test this: after a conversation, ask "If your boss asked what I'm focused on, what would you say?" If their answer surprises you, you're not aligned.

## Working with Multiple Bosses

If you have multiple bosses (direct + dotted-line, or a matrix structure):

- Complete the five conversations framework for EACH boss
- Identify where their views converge and diverge
- Balance perceived wins/losses across them
- If one boss has substantially more power, bias toward them initially — but rebalance later
- If they disagree and can't resolve via 1:1s with you, you must force them to the table together. Otherwise you get pulled apart.

> **[SRE/Platform Leader Lens: The Matrix Reality in Platform Engineering]**
>
> Platform leaders often have matrix dynamics even with a single reporting line:
> - You report to VP Engineering — but the CTO has strong opinions about infrastructure strategy
> - Product VPs don't manage you — but their satisfaction is how you're measured
> - CISO doesn't own your team — but has veto power on security-related decisions
>
> Treat each of these as a "dotted-line boss" requiring their own version of the five conversations (lighter-weight, but the same structure). Know their expectations. Know their style. Know what they need from you.

## Working at a Distance

Risk when boss is remote: falling out of step without realizing it. Compensations:

- More discipline over communication scheduling (weekly 1:1 is non-negotiable, not "when needed")
- Clearer, more comprehensive metrics (your boss needs a picture of what's happening without being present)
- At least one in-person meeting early on (even if it means travel). Face-to-face builds trust that video calls cannot.
- Find windows of your boss's time that others aren't competing for (during travel, early morning, etc.)

## Your 90-Day Plan as a Contract

> **[Core Framework: The 90-Day Plan Structure]**
>
> After ~2 weeks in the new job (enough to have initial lay of the land), create a written 90-day plan. Share with your boss. Get buy-in. Treat it as a CONTRACT — defining what you WILL do and what you WILL NOT do.
>
> | Block | Focus | Outputs | Review with boss |
> |-------|-------|---------|-----------------|
> | **Days 1-30** | Learning. Building personal credibility. NO major changes. | Situation diagnosis. Key priorities identified. Plan for next 30 days. | Day 30 review: situation + expectations conversation. Consensus on diagnosis. Buy-in to next-30 plan. |
> | **Days 31-60** | Early wins. Deepening diagnosis. Starting strategic work. | Progress on early wins. Refined assessment of strategy/structure. Initial team assessments. Resources needed for major initiatives. | Day 60 review: progress assessment. Plan for next 30 days. Resource requests. |
> | **Days 61-90** | Cementing wins. Making structural decisions. Presenting strategy. | Team changes (if needed). Strategy presented. Coalition built. Momentum visible. | Day 90 review: full assessment. Agreement on next-quarter priorities. Personal development conversation begins. |
>
> **Why written:** Forces clarity. Creates shared accountability. Prevents scope creep. Gives you something to point to when boss asks "what have you been doing?" or when priorities try to shift underneath you.

> **[Real-World Application: The 90-Day Plan Email to Your Boss]**
>
> After your first 2 weeks, send something like:
>
> *"Based on my first two weeks of learning, here's my 90-day plan. I'd like to review it together and get your input:*
>
> *Days 1-30 (remaining): [specific learning priorities, people I'm meeting, what I'm assessing]*
> *Days 31-60: [early wins I'm targeting, why they matter, what I'll need]*
> *Days 61-90: [larger initiatives I expect to propose, team/structural decisions I anticipate]*
>
> *Key risks I see: [2-3 things that could derail this]*
> *What I need from you: [specific asks — time, introductions, air cover, decisions]*
>
> *I'll send written updates biweekly and propose a 30-minute check-in at day 30 and day 60. Does this framework work for you?"*
>
> This signals: structured thinking, respect for their time, proactive communication, and clear asks. Exactly what a boss wants from a new senior hire.

## The Five Conversations with YOUR Team

> **[Core Framework: The Golden Rule of Transitions]**
>
> *"Transition others as you would wish to be transitioned yourself."*
>
> You're not just managing UP to a new boss — you ARE a new boss to your team. Apply the same five-conversations framework DOWNWARD:
>
> - **Situation:** How does each direct report see the situation? (May differ from each other and from you)
> - **Expectations:** What do YOU expect from each of them? What do THEY need from you?
> - **Resources:** What do they need to succeed that they don't currently have?
> - **Style:** How does each person prefer to communicate and be managed?
> - **Personal development:** Where does each person want to grow?
>
> Schedule the first conversation with each direct report in your first week. Introduce the framework: "I want to have structured conversations about how we work together." This signals professionalism and care — and gives you a structured way to assess your team (which feeds into Ch07: Build Your Team).

> **[Questions to Ask: The Five Conversations — Boss Direction]**
>
> When your directs haven't had a new boss in a while, they may not know what you need. Prompt them:
>
> - "What would make you say, in 6 months, that having me here improved things for you?"
> - "What's the thing you most need from me that your previous manager did or didn't provide?"
> - "How do you prefer to get feedback — in the moment, or in our 1:1s?"
> - "What's the biggest risk in your area that keeps you up at night?"
> - "Is there anything about how we work together that you'd like to flag early?"

> **[2025 Context: The Five Conversations in a Remote/Hybrid World]**
>
> Watkins assumes most boss interaction is face-to-face. Adjustments for 2025:
>
> | Conversation | 2013 assumption | 2025 reality |
> |-------------|----------------|--------------|
> | **Situation** | Discussed in person over multiple meetings | May happen entirely over video. Write a pre-read doc so boss can engage asynchronously on complex diagnosis. Don't rely on live meeting alone to build shared understanding. |
> | **Expectations** | Negotiated conversationally, confirmed verbally | Put it in writing. After every expectations conversation, send a summary email: "Here's what I understood we agreed on." Written record protects you when memories diverge later. |
> | **Resources** | Lobbied for in hallway conversations and in-person budget meetings | Build the business case as a document (not just verbal pitch). Link resources to measurable outcomes. In a remote org, the document travels farther than your personal persuasion. |
> | **Style** | Observed naturally through daily proximity | Must be explicitly discussed much earlier. You can't observe your boss's style by osmosis when you're remote. Ask directly: "How do you like to communicate? What frustrates you?" |
> | **Personal development** | Happened naturally through ongoing observation | Feedback is sparser remotely. You must actively solicit it: "What's one thing I should do differently?" — because your boss won't volunteer it as readily without daily presence cues. |
>
> **Gen AI for the five conversations:**
> - Before situation conversation: use AI to summarize public information about the business unit, recent press, competitive landscape — arrive with informed hypotheses, not blank questions
> - Before expectations conversation: draft your own "success criteria" proposal and have AI stress-test it: "What's missing from this? What might my boss care about that I haven't included?"
> - Before resource conversation: use AI to build cost/benefit models and scenario analyses quickly
> - For style conversation: analyze your boss's communication patterns (email length, Slack response time, meeting behavior) to form hypotheses before you ask directly

> **[Comparison: Neff & Citrin — Managing Up as a Senior Hire]**
>
> Neff adds to the boss relationship with specific senior-level advice:
>
> **"Don't mistake the mandate for permission."** You were hired with a mandate ("fix platform/reliability"). But a mandate from the HIRING process doesn't equal ongoing permission from the WORKING relationship. Your boss gave you the mandate when things were theoretical. Now that you're here and changes affect real people/budgets/priorities, permission must be re-earned continuously.
>
> **"Your boss is interviewing you every day for 90 days."** Every interaction — how you frame problems, what questions you ask, how you handle pressure — is still an evaluation. Act accordingly. The "honeymoon" means less criticism, not less observation.
>
> **"Find out what keeps your boss up at night."** Not what they told you in interviews (polished), but what they worry about NOW (raw). It might be: "the board is asking about reliability metrics," or "my peer VP thinks platform team is overstaffed," or "we're losing a key customer over security issues." Your early wins should address THEIR nighttime worries, not just what you find technically interesting.

> **[Checklist: Negotiate Success]**
>
> 1. How have you built relationships with new bosses in past? What worked? What didn't?
> 2. Plan the situation conversation: What issues will you raise? What do you want to say upfront?
> 3. Plan the expectations conversation: How will you figure out what's expected? What if expectations are unrealistic?
> 4. Plan the style conversation: Communication preferences? Decision-making boundaries? Update frequency?
> 5. Plan the resource conversation: What do you absolutely need? What's the cost/benefit at different resource levels?
> 6. Plan the personal development conversation: Your strengths? Development areas? What assignments would help?
> 7. How will you use the five conversations framework with YOUR direct reports?
