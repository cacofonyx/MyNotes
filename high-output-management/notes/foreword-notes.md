# Foreword to the Vintage Books Edition

> **High Output Management** — Andrew S. Grove
> *Foreword by Ben Horowitz (2015)*

Ben Horowitz — cofounder of Andreessen Horowitz and author of *The Hard Thing About Hard Things* — wrote this foreword for the 2015 Vintage Books reissue. It serves as both a tribute to Grove and a reader's guide to why the book matters. Horowitz first read *High Output Management* in 1995, at a time when "there were no blogs or TED Talks teaching us about entrepreneurship" and "there was almost nothing of use written for people like me who aspired to build and run a company." The book had "almost legendary status" in Silicon Valley — top VCs gave copies to their entrepreneurs, and aspiring leaders devoured it.

## Why This Foreword Matters

Horowitz doesn't just praise the book — he distills its core ideas in a way that previews the entire structure. For a reader approaching Grove for the first time (or returning after years), this foreword is a roadmap.

---

## Grove the Person

Horowitz opens with Grove's biography to establish credibility, but also to explain *why* the book reads the way it does:

- **Born Jewish in Hungary** during Nazi occupation, then Soviet Communist occupation
- **Arrived in New York** speaking no English, with almost no money
- **Enrolled at City College of New York**, overcame the language barrier, earned a PhD from UC Berkeley
- **Wrote a foundational semiconductor textbook** — in his non-native English — while at Fairchild Semiconductor
- **Co-founded Intel in 1968**, built it into the defining technology company of the era
- **Named *Time* magazine's Man of the Year in 1997**

Horowitz uses this background to explain Grove's writing style: *"Andy Grove was all substance."* The paperback cover photo showed Grove standing by the Intel sign in work clothes, key card still hanging from his belt. No designer suit, no power pose. *"He did not have time for pretty photo shoots or self-promotion."*

> **[Core Concept: The Man Behind the Method]**
>
> Grove's biography isn't incidental — it shaped his management philosophy. A person who rebuilt himself from zero in a foreign country, mastering a new language well enough to write textbooks in it, brings a particular worldview to management: **nothing is given; everything is earned through disciplined effort; the only thing that matters is output, not appearance.** This worldview permeates the entire book. Grove never wastes a sentence on motivation-speak or inspirational platitudes. Every concept is operational: *here's how it works, here's how to do it, here's what goes wrong if you don't.*
>
> **For Senior EMs:** Grove's immigrant hustle is worth internalizing not as a "work harder" message, but as a *substance over signaling* message. In a world of management theater — impressive-sounding frameworks, lengthy strategy decks, leadership offsites with no follow-through — Grove's approach is ruthlessly practical. Does it produce output? Then do it. Does it not? Then stop.

## The Three Core Ideas Horowitz Highlights

Horowitz identifies three aspects of the book's "genius":

### 1. Brevity of Insight

> *"In as little as one sentence, it lucidly explains concepts that require entire books from lesser writers."*

Horowitz gives examples throughout the foreword of Grove's one-sentence insights that carry the weight of entire management philosophies.

### 2. Original Ideas or New Angles on Old Ones

> *"It consistently uncovers brand-new management ideas or finds new insights into old standards."*

Grove doesn't just repeat received wisdom. He either discovers new frameworks (managerial leverage, task-relevant maturity) or takes existing ideas (meetings, performance reviews) and reveals dimensions nobody else had articulated.

### 3. Teaching Greatness, Not Competence

> *"While most management books attempt to teach basic competency, High Output Management teaches the reader how to be great."*

This is the key distinction. Most management books teach you how to not be terrible. Grove assumes you're already competent and teaches you how to be *excellent*. The difference: competent managers run meetings; excellent managers understand that the *type* of meeting determines its function and design it accordingly. Competent managers give performance reviews; excellent managers understand that the review is a *leverage point* for the entire organization's output.

> **[Modern Lens: Why This Book Resurged in the 2010s-2020s]**
>
> *High Output Management* was published in 1983, went somewhat quiet, then exploded in popularity after this 2015 reissue. Why?
>
> Several forces converged:
> - **The rise of "engineering management" as a discipline** — as tech companies scaled, the need for management practices rooted in production thinking (not MBA generalities) became acute. Grove's approach — treat management as manufacturing — resonated with engineers-turned-managers.
> - **Ben Horowitz's influence** — a16z became the most prominent VC firm in tech. Horowitz's endorsement put the book into the hands of every portfolio company founder.
> - **The SRE and DevOps movements** — both rooted in production/operations thinking. Grove's factory metaphor is the intellectual ancestor of "you build it, you run it."
> - **Disillusionment with "servant leadership" platitudes** — many managers found that well-intentioned but vague frameworks ("be a coach," "empower your team") lacked operational specificity. Grove's book fills that gap with *how*, not just *what*.
>
> **The irony:** Grove wrote the book for middle managers at Intel in 1983. Forty years later, it's the management bible for Silicon Valley executives. The fundamentals haven't changed because human organizations haven't changed — only the technology and vocabulary have.

## Key Quotes Horowitz Extracts

Horowitz pulls specific quotes that preview the book's major concepts:

### The Manager's Output Equation

> *"A manager's output = the output of his organization + the output of the neighboring organizations under his influence."*

Horowitz explains: *"A manager's skills and knowledge are only valuable if she uses them to get more leverage from her people. So, Ms. Manager, you know more about our product's viral loop than anyone in the company? That's worth exactly nothing unless you can effectively transfer that knowledge to the rest of the organization."*

> **[Senior EM Application: The Output Equation for Your Role]**
>
> This equation — which Grove will develop fully in Chapter 3 — is the single most important definition of management in the book. For a Senior EM, it means:
>
> Your output is NOT:
> - How many hours you work
> - How many meetings you attend
> - How many Slack messages you send
> - How smart your strategy docs are
> - How impressive your presentations look
>
> Your output IS:
> - The total output of the 2-4 teams under your supervision
> - Plus: the output improvements you drive in neighboring teams you influence (product teams you partner with, platform teams you collaborate with, other engineering orgs you share practices with)
>
> This reframing is liberating and terrifying in equal measure. Liberating because it means you don't have to do everything yourself. Terrifying because it means your personal brilliance is irrelevant if your teams aren't producing.

### Motivation and Training

> *"When a person is not doing his job, there can only be two reasons for it. The person either can't do it or won't do it; he is either not capable or not motivated."*

Horowitz emphasizes the implication: *"All you can do to improve the output of an employee is motivate and train. There is nothing else."*

> **[Core Concept: The Two-Lever Model — Can't vs. Won't]**
>
> This is Grove's razor for diagnosing performance problems. Before you restructure teams, change processes, add tooling, or escalate — ask: **Is this person unable (capability gap) or unwilling (motivation gap)?** The intervention is completely different:
>
> | Diagnosis | Root Cause | Intervention | Wrong Intervention |
> |-----------|-----------|-------------|-------------------|
> | **Can't do it** (capability) | Lacks skills, knowledge, experience, or resources | Training, coaching, pairing, providing resources, adjusting scope to match capability | Motivational speeches, incentives, pressure — these don't help someone who literally doesn't know how |
> | **Won't do it** (motivation) | Lacks motivation, engagement, alignment, or sees no reason to try | Understanding what drives them, connecting work to purpose, removing demotivators, addressing cultural issues | More training — this is insulting to someone who *can* do the work but has lost the will |
>
> **The SRE example:** An engineer's on-call response times are consistently slow. Is it because they don't know the systems well enough to respond quickly (can't → train them, improve runbooks, pair them with a senior)? Or because they're burned out, feel unsupported, or don't believe on-call matters (won't → address the motivation, reduce toil, show them the customer impact)?

### Planning and Today's Gaps

> *"I have seen far too many people who upon recognizing today's gap try very hard to determine what decision has to be made to close it. But today's gap represents a failure of planning sometime in the past."*

Horowitz adds: *"If you only understand one thing about building products, you must understand that energy put in early in the process pays off tenfold and energy put in at the end of the program pays off negative tenfold."*

> **[SRE Lens: Incident Response vs. Incident Prevention]**
>
> Grove's planning insight applies directly to reliability: **today's outage is a failure of engineering decisions made months ago.** The P1 incident that consumes your team for 6 hours — the root cause isn't the failed deployment, it's the architectural decision 18 months ago that created a single point of failure, or the decision 6 months ago to defer the migration that would have added redundancy, or the decision 3 months ago to skip the load test that would have revealed the capacity gap.
>
> This doesn't mean incidents are someone's "fault." It means the *energy* investment curve applies to reliability: investing in architecture, redundancy, and testing *early* pays off 10x. Investing in incident response *after* the outage pays off negative 10x (you're already losing money, customers, and trust).
>
> **Practical implication:** If you find your team spending >50% of time on reactive work (incidents, firefighting, urgent fixes), the problem is not today's incidents — it's the planning (or lack thereof) from the past. The fix is not "respond faster" — it's "invest in prevention so there's less to respond to."

### Task-Relevant Maturity

Horowitz calls out the task-relevant maturity concept as having personally shaped his management approach. He describes the interview question it inspired: *"Is it better to be a hands-on or hands-off manager?"*

> *"It seems like a simple enough question, but it sorts out the 95 percent of managers who never think deeply about their craft from the 5 percent who do."*

The answer is: it depends on the employee's maturity *for the specific task*. Hands-on for low task-relevant maturity; hands-off for high. Grove illustrates with a powerful quote: *"The subordinate did poor work. My associate's reaction: 'He has to make his own mistakes. That's how he learns!' The problem with this is that the subordinate's tuition is paid by his customers."*

> **[Senior EM Application: The "It Depends" Answer Is the Right Answer]**
>
> If you're interviewing for a Director role and someone asks "Are you hands-on or hands-off?", the *only* right answer is "It depends." Then explain *what* it depends on (the person's experience with the specific task) and *how* you calibrate (you start more hands-on, build trust, then pull back as they demonstrate competence).
>
> The people who answer "hands-off — I hire smart people and get out of their way" are abdicating. The people who answer "hands-on — I need to be in the details" are micromanaging. Grove's framework is the escape from this false binary.

## The Bookshelf Story

Horowitz closes with two personal anecdotes that reveal Grove's character:

**First:** When Horowitz told Grove he loved the book, Grove's response was *"Why?"* — not "thank you." *"He was always teaching and always expecting more from every student."*

**Second:** Grove's bookshelf story — he ran out of space, decided to throw away management books rather than buy a bigger house, but couldn't bring himself to discard the kind inscriptions. So he tore out the inscription pages, saved them, and threw away the books. *"I have a large stack of pages of nice notes to me and plenty of space for good books."*

> **[Core Concept: The Standard for Substance]**
>
> The bookshelf story is funny but also reveals Grove's filter: **does this contain real insight, or is it filler?** He kept the human gesture (the inscription) and discarded the content that didn't meet his bar. This is the standard Grove holds himself to throughout *High Output Management* — every sentence earns its place or it doesn't exist. It's also the standard he implicitly sets for managers: is what you're doing producing real output, or is it organizational filler?

## The Optimism Paradox

Horowitz shares a final Grove insight from a 2001 conversation about CEOs missing their numbers:

> *"CEOs always act on leading indicators of good news, but only act on lagging indicators of bad news."*

When Horowitz asked why, Grove explained:

> *"In order to build anything great, you have to be an optimist, because by definition you are trying to do something that most people would consider impossible. Optimists most certainly do not listen to leading indicators of bad news."*

When Horowitz suggested Grove write about this, Grove refused: *"Why would I do that? It would be a waste of time to write about how to not follow human nature."*

> **[Mental Model: The Optimism Trap for Senior Leaders]**
>
> This is one of the most profound observations in the foreword, and it applies directly to SRE leadership:
>
> - **You act quickly on leading indicators of good news:** Latency improved? Celebrate. Deployment frequency up? Report it. Error budget healthy? Declare success.
> - **You act slowly on lagging indicators of bad news:** Attrition creeping up? "It's probably just a blip." Toil percentage rising? "We'll address it next quarter." Incident rate climbing? "Each one was a special case."
>
> Grove's insight explains *why* this happens: you *have to* be an optimist to lead — you're building things most people think are impossible. But that same optimism blinds you to warning signs.
>
> **The corrective:** Build systems that force you to confront leading indicators of bad news *before* you'd naturally act on them:
> - SLO burn-rate alerts that page when the trend is bad, not when the threshold is breached
> - Quarterly attrition reviews with explicit "what are we ignoring?" discussions
> - Toil tracking with hard thresholds that trigger action (Google's 50% rule)
> - "Pre-mortem" exercises: "Imagine this initiative failed in 6 months. Why?"
>
> These systems work because they externalize the bad-news detection that optimistic leaders naturally resist. Grove wouldn't write about this because he thought it was fighting human nature. But you can *engineer around* human nature — which is, ironically, exactly what Grove teaches throughout the rest of the book.
